Kindred Hours

Amongst the blaring honk along Yonge street, the hopelessly delayed TTC, and the uncoordinated sidewalks of what we call home in Toronto, it can get lonely. Layering on top the precarity of modern careers against the backdrop of our endless ambitions and manufactured sense of control for the future, it almost feels like we’re ruthlessly cornered. We attend intellectual or professional events and try out new hobbies alone, convincing ourselves that we have learned to enjoy solitude, but also secretly wished that we can share the joy collectively.

We yearn for a different way of being than what is prescribed. An act of resistance towards the hyper-productivity and the endless doomscrolling of dopamine-inducing reels. We didn’t like how the world is becoming less sociable and wished that there are options beyond run clubs to meet new people and build a tribe.

We want to spend kindred hours with people of great characters.

We read difficult books as an act of resistance to AI slop. We learn new skills and teach one another as an act of resistance to endless scrolling. We hold deep conversations as an act of resistance to small talk. We do physically challenging things as an act of resistance to a life lived entirely on screens. We linger over meals, notice the changing seasons, and enjoy the small things as an act of resistance to the idea that every hour must be optimized. Not to become better versions of ourselves, but to feel more fully alive—and to replenish our souls in good company.

How we spend our kindred hours

  1. Come as a person.

    Be curious about who someone is, not just what they do. Be interested, not impressive—there’s nothing you need to prove here.

  2. Make room for one another.

    Open the circle when someone is standing outside it. Enjoy familiar faces, but seek out someone new. Share the conversation rather than dominate it.

  3. Offer attention.

    Keep phones tucked away, except when needed for accessibility, caregiving, or urgent matters. Listen fully, and ask before offering ideas or solutions.

  4. Go deeper without pushing.

    Share at your own pace; passing is always welcome. Respect boundaries, protect private stories, and ask before photographing or posting about others.

  5. Contribute without turning belonging into work.

    Help in small ways that fit your capacity. Honour commitments and communicate when plans change, but don’t feel obliged to attend everything.

  6. A shared purpose, clearly communicated.

    Each gathering centres on a book, meal, skill, question, creative project, or physical challenge.

  7. A welcome and a few thoughtful introductions.

    A host helps you settle in. Nametags pair first names with conversation starters—not job titles. Suggested connections give you people to meet without making the evening a networking checklist.

  8. Small groups and something to do together.

    We often begin in pairs or small circles, using a playful prompt or shared activity to ease introductions. Groups may rotate so meeting new people doesn’t depend on confidently working the room.

  9. Guided conversation, with room to breathe.

    Questions deepen gradually, sometimes following quiet reading or journaling. Hosts make space for everyone, allow passing, and address boundary violations. We also leave time for unstructured conversation and unexpected connections.

  10. Shared responsibility and a proper ending.

    Small, voluntary roles rotate among members. We close with a reflection or appreciation.
